The BIG Picture

 

The ad above appeared in a Sears flyer dated 10/17/02, inserted into the Gainesville Sun. It would be a mistake to read too much into any intent behind the ad, but it can be a useful and entertaining exercise to analyze what "Joe Six-Pack" might infer from the presentation, and how this might influence his decision to invest in an HDTV monitor.

Let's begin by examining exactly how large each of the displays above actually are, rather than how they appear relative to one another in the ad. The size of a display is given as the screen diagonal, regardless of the screen's aspect ratio. Thus a traditional 4:3 aspect ratio display with a 36" diagonal has a screen height of 21.6 inches and screen width of 28.8 inches. A new 43" diagonal widescreen display with a 16:9 aspect ratio has a screen height of 21.1 inches and a screen width of 37.5 inches. Thus the screen heights of the displays are virtually identical, and a 4:3 image should appear to be virtually the same size on both.

As the picture and the graphic inset on the 43" screen indicate, the 16:9 picture has additional picture information on the sides. But the graphic suggests that the 4:3 screen has a bit more information on the top and bottom, which is only true in certain modes of the widescreen display, used fill the widescreen display when the source is a traditional 4:3 image; and this mode will not show more information on the sides as is the case in this image. The corollary is that if the source is 16:9 a 4:3 screen must either crop off the sides of the image or present the entire image in "letterbox" mode.

It is likely that these are not actual screen shots, but rather, simulated images added to the photography by an artist. The artist appears to have kept the size of the objects in the images the same; the widescreen display shows more information on the sides. But the top and bottom of the widescreen images are slightly cropped. Chances are that "Joe" is not going to notice this in the ad; but he probably will notice the differences in the showroom.

In this image the 36 inch display looks larger than the 43 inch display. One reason for this is the positioning of the displays, with the 4:3 display in the foreground. This positioning is slightly deceptive, since both displays have virtually the same screen heights. But in the showroom, it is common for people to perceive that a 4:3 display looks BIGGER than a 16:9 display with the same screen height. Perhaps this has something to do with the price tags; the price of a widescreen display is always BIGGER, whether the 4:3 display is a new HDTV monitor or a traditional interlaced NTSC receiver.

All of this makes one wonder what message Sears is trying to convey. The profit margins are much larger on HDTV monitors than mass market NTSC receivers. Maybe Sears makes up the difference with volume?

Bottom line, the BIG MESSAGE in this ad is that BIG SCREEN NTSC receivers are a much better value than their HDTV counterparts. You can buy the 36" TV and the Home Theater in a Box surround sound system with DVD player and pocket the $500 difference. And that $699 TV has component video inputs that make DVD movies look almost as good as DVDs on an HDTV monitor.
