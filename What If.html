WHAT IF?

The televised images of of Lee Harvey Oswald's "execution" at the hands of Jack Ruby, which took place on November 24, 1963 are still burned into the national consciousness. It helped to define the medium of television for millions of Americans who were watching events unfold that Saturday afternoon.

Two days earlier, a 15 year old boy sat in a high school biology class when the announcement came across the PA system that the President of the United States had been shot and killed. The reality of the assassination of John F. Kennedy was sinking in to the national consciousness that afternoon. Forty-eight hours later, infatuated by the spectacle unfolding on his parents' black and white television, the boy was draw in by the power of this new medium.

The images had turned to that of the fallen President's son and his widow, touching the casket that was lying in state in the Capitol Rotunda. Then the scene shifted to Texas as Oswald was being transferred from the Dallas jail. We witnessed the execution of the assassin on live television. The nation was assured that justice had been done before the images of Kennedy's funeral were burned into our collective memories.

Thirty eight years later, we were drawn to the TV again, this time by reports of a plane that had crashed into the World Trade Center. The vivid progressive-scan pictures on my DTV monitor showed gaping holes in the upper floors of one tower of the WTC, and massive amounts of smoke billowing into the sky around the tower, which had been delivering broadcasts as usual from nine of New York's analog television stations, five DTV stations, and four FM radio stations.

Then the unimaginable happened. There before our eyes ANOTHER plane flew into the second WTC tower. The instant replays showed the jet bank at the last moment; not to avoid the tower, but to maximize the devastation by spreading the force of the impact and the fuel among multiple floors of the building.

Later, a father came home to talk with his 15 year old daughter about the day's events. She was sitting in biology when the announcement came to turn on all classroom TVs. Throughout the day she moved from class to class, never far from the images on the screens; the teachers and students were drawn into the spectacle. He learned that she was watching when the second jet hit the WTC; and when the towers fell.

More images permanently etched in the national consciousness.

 

Is The Medium The Message?

Over the years there have been many debates about the Kennedy assassination - even a movie with a Hollywood spin on the story. It remains a haunting memory, a reminder of the awesome power of television to sweep away the physical limitations of our senses - to be anywhere, anytime, when history is made.

That this was a major factor in the planning of the terrorist attack on the World Trade Center is hardly in doubt. It is an uncomfortable reality that the terrorists were able to co-opt the very media they despise to deliver their message.

Joan Deppa, a communications professor at Syracuse University's Newhouse School, told Broadcasting and Cable, "Terrorism has become an act of mass communications." Deppa co-authored a book on the media and Pan Am Flight 103, downed by terrorists in Scotland. The second plane, she said, was "...meant to crash in front of us. The first plane that came in was to get the attention of the media."

 

A New Priority for Digital Broadcasting?

In the aftermath of the attacks, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) requested that broadcast stations suspend EAS tests. Ironically, EAS had evolved from a Cold War-era system designed to prevent high powered broadcast towers from being used as homing beacons for enemy air and missile attacks. The FEMA announcement was a tacit acknowledgement that in times of national emergency, the high powered, big stick approach to broadcasting was a point of vulnerability, and could not be relied upon to deliver emergency messages to the public.

In the aftermath of the recent WTC attack, the vulnerability of current analog and future digital broadcast networks has once again been called into question. Of the major TV outlets in New York, only WCBS-TV remained on the air, after switching to an auxiliary transmitter on the Empire State Building (ESB); WCBS-DT also continued to broadcast from the ESB.

The day after the attack, Stephen W. Long, Director, Motion Imagery Program Office, and Chair of multiple Department of Defense groups involved with high resolution imaging, issued a request through the OpenDTV e-mail list to determine the feasibility of installing COFDM-based single frequency networks in New York City. The intended purpose was to provide secure wireless data broadcasts to emergency workers and law enforcement agencies. Long indicated that the transmission infrastructure could have been in place in a matter of days, but that receivers with the ability to provide data links to notebook computers were difficult to locate. Suitable receivers have now been located and efforts are underway to construct a network in New York to demonstrate the feasibility of using COFDM for secure wireless datacasting in response to this and future national emergencies.

In December of 1999, A group chaired by Long came out against the 8VSB modulation system employed in the U.S. DTV standard. On 12/03/99 Electronic Engineering Times reported: "The Pentagon has come out against the U.S. digital TV standard's vestigial sideband modulation scheme and is pressing the Federal Communications Commission to reopen the debate over use of an alternative approach called coded orthogonal frequency division multiplex (COFDM)."

"A Department of Defense video working group said it has concerns about "potential problems" with the 8-VSB approach and wants the FCC to reconsider the COFDM system that's used in Europe. Specifically, the group said it has concerns "...about our national capability to employ digital television broadcast systems to communicate with the public during civil and defense emergencies."

The concerns expressed by the working group in 1999 primarily centered around the difficulty in receiving 8VSB broadcasts and the need for outdoor antennas. The ability of COFDM to support mobile and portable receivers with low complexity antennas was viewed as an important benefit from a national security perspective.

Over the past two years, the benefits of moving to networks of transmitters using the same frequencies - as is the case today for cellular telephony - have captured the interest of broadcasters worldwide. Known as single frequency networks (SFNs), this approach to digital broadcasting offers a number of benefits that may rekindle interest in COFDM in the aftermath of the terrorist attacks.

In fact, it may be fair to say that there was still a high level of interest in this technology among U.S. broadcasters prior to the attacks. A recent study shows that US TV stations are still interested in COFDM over 8VSB, despite the statements of the FCC supporting 8VSB.

 

WHAT IF there were SFNs in New York NOW?

It may be a useful exercise for U.S. broadcasters, and especially the FCC and Congress, to consider for a moment what would be different today had the decision been made two years ago to allow broadcasters to build COFDM based SFNs.

Here are a few WHAT IF scenarios:

  • Survivability - presumably all broadcasters would still be on the air. With multiple transmitters serving a market, the loss of a single transmitter would not be catastrophic.

  • Data broadcasting - in the aftermath of the attacks 90% of Americans surveyed turned first to television for news. The Internet was also swamped with people desperate for information. Based on personal discussions, many people were already at work, and the Internet or radio were their only sources of information. The major Internet news portals had to rethink their coverage to deal with the crush of traffic. High resolution images and streaming media files were abandoned; in their place most coverage resorted to HTML text, which allowed many more people to access these sites. This points to a major limitation of the Internet model; it is the inverse of broadcasting - millions of people attempting to access a few key sources of information. With data broadcasting capabilities, television broadcasters could have provided a wealth of high quality digital media services alongside the linear narrative broadcasts. And much of this data could have been linked to the narrative broadcasts in the form of interactive services, which could have provided more depth of coverage.

  • Accessibility - with appropriate use of SFNs, many classes of portable receiving devices would have had access to wireless digital broadcasts. Workers in offices with computers equipped to receive these broadcasts (both TV and data) would have had access to more information. Perhaps more lives would have been saved in the WTC towers if workers, most of which were sitting in front of a computer, were able to access these broadcasts.

  • The Aftermath - many conventional forms of communication were rendered nearly useless by the N.Y. attack. Of the 500,000 telephone lines in lower Manhattan, 200,000 were knocked out. Many cellular telephone transceivers were knocked out and the remaining networks were overwhelmed. And in the nation's largest television market, broadcasters were thankful that most of their viewers had cable. With SFNs, New York broadcasters would still be on the air. And the network would have been in place to deliver the information needed by the public, emergency workers and law enforcement to a variety of wireless information appliances.
